The recently passed Lindsey Graham Act gives President Trump all the authority required to make this deal. The bill has several elements that are immensely useful for President Trump, beginning with the fact that the baseline of the bill sits upon the cornerstone of current U.S. sanctions against Russia. If you take away the originating Russian sanctions, HR5334 disappears. The bill grants Trump the ability to place 100% tariffs on the top five trading partners of Russia, but the bill also grants wide latitude as to how those top five are determined. Top five oil, gas, financial, etc... specific (energy, grain, oil, minerals etc.) the actual parameters or definitions of the “top five” are undetermined except by monetary value. There is no minimum tariff but there is a maximum up to 100% within 30 days on all goods imported into the US from the five largest importers of Russian crude oil or gas as well as any country that knowingly made new purchases of oil or gas 30 days after the law was enacted or was among the top five countries helping Russia evade sanctions.
But there’s the key, again the authority is based on the pre-existing “sanctions.” So, who determines sanction relief? It turns out interim, temporary, or strategic relief, in coordination with the policy objectives of the President... that would be U.S. Secretary of Treasury Scott Bessent. The law allows for implementation per executive order, which the general license is derived from - which is to say the general license may be legal but not consistent with the intent of the law. The other question is on CAATSA, because a sustained commitment to the scope of the general license would appear to be a significant change in U.S. sanctions policy to Russia, which CAATSA does not authorize the president to make unilaterally. And, of course, the admin may say the policy hasn’t been significantly changed because in the end only a small amount of diesel can be imported due to constraints from Russia.
